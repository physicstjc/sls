WHY DOES IT TURN?

Same colours. Changing current.

The red lead is connected to the cell’s positive terminal; the black lead is negative. The red and black coil halves rotate with the split ring.

When the red half meets the brush fed by the red lead, conventional current runs out from the commutator along that half. Half a turn later it meets the negative brush, so current runs back into the commutator.

The opposite forces form a couple. Commutation reverses the current relative to the coloured halves every half-turn, keeping the turning effect in the same sense.

THREE FINGERS. THREE DIRECTIONS.

Your left hand is the key.

  1. First finger → magnetic field. Point from the north pole to the south pole.
  2. Second finger → conventional current. Point in the direction of positive charge flow, not electron flow.
  3. Thumb → force. This gives the force on the highlighted current-carrying wire.

Arrange the three fingers at right angles. In Practise, the model faces you and the rotor is held at the angle you choose. Use the orientation slider to compare 0° and 180°, then match the target card and hold steady.

Follow the contacts, not the colours. The red supply lead stays positive and the black lead stays negative. The coil’s red and black halves keep their colours as they turn. When the red half contacts the positive brush, current runs out from the rear commutator along that half. After half a turn it contacts the negative brush and current runs into the commutator. At the insulating gaps, current briefly stops. Swapping brush connections moves the lead endpoints while retaining their cell polarity.

Depth matters: ⊙ means toward you; ⊗ means away from you. Keep the middle finger slightly angled so its joints remain visible. A single camera estimates depth and can be wrong when fingers overlap.

Confirm your left hand once the camera finds it. This calibration avoids relying on camera mirroring conventions. Reconfirm after switching cameras. Show just one hand.

This is camera-overlay AR: the 3D motor is anchored to the screen, not a detected table. Phone movement does not place it in the room. Model rotation is illustrative; supply strength controls animation speed, not a calibrated RPM.

Tracking is a practice aid, not a grade. If feedback seems wrong, spread the fingers, improve lighting, and try another view. Ring and little fingers are not assessed.
